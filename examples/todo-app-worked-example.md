# Chief Product Partner worked example: a to-do app

**SIMULATED EXAMPLE.** Every person, interview, count, observation, test result, and decision below is invented to show how discovery works. This is not research about students or proof that a to-do app should be built. There is no live project behind this example.

The starting idea is simple: “I want to build a to-do app.” We use one fictional group, university students managing assignments, and follow the idea through nine steps. The same evidence carries through the whole example.

## 1. Frame: what decision are we making?

**We do:** Decide whose tasks we mean and what could improve. For now, focus on students who handle several assignment deadlines at once. Ask whether they actually miss work because they cannot keep track of it.

**Sample output:** “Should we help students keep track of assignments? We have an app idea, but no evidence yet that students need another tool. We would look for missed or late work, how students currently track it, and whether a simpler change would help.”

## 2. Understand: where does the problem happen?

**We do:** Follow a recent assignment from when it was announced to when it was submitted. Separate forgetting the task from starting too late, underestimating the work, or not knowing the deadline.

**Sample output:** “A reminder might help someone who forgets a deadline. It will do less for someone who knows the deadline but puts off starting. We need to see which situation actually happens.”

## 3. Research: see what students do

**We do:** Check any existing course announcements and assignment lists the students use. Ask students to show their last few assignments, their calendar or notes, and what they submitted. Include students who seem organized as well as those who report missed work. We do not pitch our app first.

**SIMULATED findings:** We speak with 12 students recruited from one course over two weeks. Seven show at least one assignment they started later than intended. Five already record deadlines in a calendar. Four missed a deadline; three of those four say they knew the date but lost track of when to begin. Two say course instructions changed after they copied a deadline. These groups overlap. One course and a small volunteer sample cannot tell us how common this is across a university.

## 4. Make sense of what we found

**We do:** Compare stories with the assignment history. Look for exceptions: students who use a calendar but still start late, and students who never use one but submit on time.

**Sample output:** “The stronger problem in this small group is starting work early enough, not merely storing a due date. A basic list may duplicate what five students already have. We should test whether showing the next small action helps them begin.” This remains a hypothesis, not a measured population-wide need.

## 5. State the bets

**We do:** Say what must be true for a product to help, and what would prove us wrong.

**Sample output:**

- Students can break an assignment into a next action without a tutor doing it for them. If they cannot, a blank to-do list may add work.
- Seeing that action at the right time helps them start. If they still delay, reminders alone are weak.
- They will return to the tool after the first week. If they abandon it, an existing calendar or course page may be enough.

## 6. Explore ways to help

**We do:** Compare a few responses before deciding to build.

| Option | What it could help with | Trade-off |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Better assignment checklist in the course page | Keeps work where students already look | Depends on course staff changing the page |
| Calendar template with start dates | Fast to try with an existing tool | Students must set it up and keep it current |
| Small to-do app with a next action and due date | Gives one clear place to choose what to do today | Another app to maintain; duplicate deadlines can drift |

**Sample output:** “Try the calendar template and a small clickable to-do prototype side by side. If the template works just as well, we have no reason to build an app.”

## 7. Test before deciding

**We do:** Set decision rules first. In a **SIMULATED** two-week test, 12 students from a different course use a prototype for real assignments. A comparison group of 12 uses the calendar template. Both groups have similar assignment loads in this fictional setup; assignment difficulty and self-selection still limit the comparison.

**Rules set before results:** At least 8 of 12 prototype users must create a next action and due date without help; at least 7 must begin an assignment before the final day; at least 6 must use the tool in both weeks. The prototype must show a clear advantage over the template on early starts to justify building more.

**SIMULATED results:** Ten of 12 prototype users set up a task without help; 8 begin early; 7 use it in both weeks. In the calendar group, 8 begin early. The prototype clears its three usage rules but shows **no advantage on early starts**. The small test cannot show that the app improves outcomes.

## 8. Decide what to do next

**We do:** Compare every result with the rules and name what would change the decision.

**SIMULATED decision brief:** “Do not build the app yet. Students could use the prototype, but the calendar template produced the same number of early starts. Try the cheaper template in two more courses. Ask course staff whether they can add suggested start dates to assignment notices. Reconsider an app only if the existing approach repeatedly fails on a defined task that the prototype solves better.” The product manager owns the decision and the follow-up date.

## 9. Describe the build, if warranted

**We do:** Stop here for this example. The simulated decision does not support an app, so there is no PRD. If a later test shows that an app helps students start earlier than a calendar template, we can write a first-version brief linked to that evidence. It would need to cover adding a task, due date, and next action, as well as changed deadlines and students who stop using reminders.

**Where a real project would stand:** If you bring this idea without research, we begin at Step 1. The numbers above belong only to this simulated walkthrough. The next useful move is to ask a specific group to show how they handled their last few tasks.
